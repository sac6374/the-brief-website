A Jobs Miss Just Took September Off the Table — For Now

Wall Street ended last week at record highs, but the catalyst was an ugly one: the U.S. economy shed 23,000 jobs in July, the first monthly payroll decline since February. Traders read the miss as a green light — fewer jobs means less inflation pressure, which means the Fed probably won't hike in September. This week, that thesis gets its first real test: July CPI drops Wednesday, and one hot number could flip the script entirely.

00 Smart in 30 Seconds
The whole issue in a paragraph

The U.S. economy unexpectedly shed 23,000 jobs in July — the first monthly payroll decline since February — and Wall Street celebrated. The S&P 500 closed at a fresh record of 7,757.64 (+0.62%) because traders read the miss as a Fed-killer: September rate-hike odds collapsed from 58% to 44% on the data, the 10-year Treasury yield fell 7 basis points to 4.60%, and tech led the charge, with the Nasdaq gaining 1.30%. Palantir was the week's standout story, surging 35% in five sessions after Q2 revenue of $1.94 billion smashed the $1.80 billion consensus. This morning, S&P 500 futures are barely changed (+0.13%), WTI crude is climbing back toward $80 as Iran-Oman Hormuz talks stall, and the week's defining event — July CPI — doesn't drop until Wednesday. One hot inflation print and the jobs-miss trade unravels fast.

01 What Moved Markets Today

Jobs Shocker, Palantir Surge, and a Fifth S&P Record in One Week

Macro — July Nonfarm Payrolls

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that nonfarm payrolls fell by 23,000 in July, a massive miss versus the Dow Jones consensus of +83,000 and the first monthly decline since February. The unemployment rate actually ticked down to 4.1% from 4.2%, but the headline number dominated: markets immediately priced out the Fed hike, sending Treasury yields lower and equities higher. The BLS also revised combined May and June payrolls down by 103,000 — making the labor market picture meaningfully weaker than previously understood.

Equities — Tech & AI Leaders

Palantir rose 8.9% on Friday alone, capping a 35% weekly gain after its Q2 earnings blowout. SpaceX surged 15.8% on the week, aided by an Argus Research upgrade to Buy following strong first-quarter results as a newly public company — revenue of $7.8 billion came in well above the $6.93 billion analyst estimate, up 92% YoY. Cloudflare added 8% on Friday after beating Q2 revenue expectations. On the Dow, Salesforce (+2.47%) and Nvidia (+2.33%) led; Visa (−2.20%) and Chevron (−1.49%) lagged.

Overnight & Premarket — Monday, Aug 10

U.S. equity futures are fractionally positive heading into Monday's open: S&P 500 E-minis +0.13%, Nasdaq 100 E-minis +0.25%, Dow E-minis −0.02%. Asian markets were mixed overnight, with the Nikkei slipping 0.12% and the Hang Seng up 0.54%. WTI crude is climbing toward $80 per barrel — up roughly 2.9% on the session — as Iran denied holding direct talks with the U.S. and reiterated demands including lifting of its naval blockade before any Hormuz agreement. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack is scheduled to speak today; markets will parse every word for rate-path signals ahead of Wednesday's CPI.

02 Why It Mattered

The Market Is Pricing in a Soft Landing — But the Data Is Telling a Murkier Story

On the surface, Friday was a clean risk-on day: weak jobs, yields fall, stocks rise. But the details are more complicated. The unemployment rate dropped to 4.1% from 4.2% — which on its own signals a stable labor market — while the headline payroll decline was driven partly by seasonal distortions and strike-related activity. That's the ambiguity Fed Chair Kevin Warsh inherits as he navigates his first months leading a central bank that has spent two years fighting stubborn inflation. Markets are betting he stays on hold. But the Fed may read the 4.1% unemployment rate as a sign the labor market isn't broken — meaning the inflation fight isn't over.

The broader backdrop matters here. The 10-year yield had climbed 30 basis points during July alone, reaching a year-to-date high of 4.74% as energy-driven inflation fears mounted during the Iran conflict. Friday's 7-basis-point decline to 4.60% was a relief valve — but yields remain historically elevated, and oil is already reversing course this morning. The September rate-hike probability sits at 44%, which means the market is essentially a coin-flip on whether Warsh moves. Wednesday's CPI is no longer just a data point — it's the deciding vote.

Context: The labor force participation rate edged down to 61.4% in July, now off 0.7 percentage point this year alone as nearly 1.4 million people have exited the workforce. The headline unemployment rate looks better partly because fewer people are counted as looking for work — a dynamic the Fed will not ignore.

04 What It Means

A Data-Dependent Week With One Dominant Question: Does the Rally Survive CPI?

For Rates

The 10-year yield at 4.60% reflects a market that has dialed back near-term hike expectations but hasn't abandoned them. At 44% probability for a September hike, rates markets are effectively balanced. A CPI print above consensus on Wednesday tips the balance hawkishly — potentially re-pricing the 10-year back toward the 4.70%+ range seen in late July.

For Equities

The S&P 500 is up 21.85% YoY and trading at record highs with an implied 2026 year-end target of 8,050 from CFRA. Those targets assume no September hike and continued earnings momentum. Tech in particular — up nearly 24% YTD — is most exposed if yields reprice higher. Conversely, a soft CPI print could push the S&P toward 7,800 relatively quickly.

For the Economy

Two consecutive months of downward payroll revisions totaling 103,000, combined with a falling labor-force participation rate, signal that the labor market is losing momentum even if the headline unemployment rate looks stable. That's a stagflation-lite scenario — growth slowing while energy-driven inflation (WTI near $80) remains sticky — which is the worst outcome for both bonds and equities.

What to Watch

Wednesday's July CPI is the week's pivot point. Beyond that: OPEC's monthly oil market report also drops Wednesday alongside IEA data, which will directly shape the energy-inflation narrative. Earnings today from Barrick Mining, Rocket Lab, Hims & Hers Health, and Ferguson Enterprises add micro color. Cleveland Fed President Hammack speaks today — first Fed voice since the jobs report.

Key Watch Item

July CPI (Wednesday, Aug 12, 8:30 a.m. ET): consensus expects core CPI at approximately +0.2% MoM. Any reading at or above +0.3% MoM would likely restore September hike bets to majority odds and force a significant re-rating of rate-sensitive equities.

06 Finance Career Lens

What a Jobs Miss, a Rate-Hike Coin Flip, and Record Stocks Mean for Your Recruiting Story

Across all tracks

This week crystallizes a skill every finance interviewer tests: the ability to hold two contradictory ideas simultaneously. Equities are at records. The economy shed jobs. Both things are true — and understanding why requires a working knowledge of Fed reaction functions, real rates, and earnings drivers that goes beyond headline numbers.

Wealth Management & Private Banking

Clients holding 60/40 portfolios are watching a confusing moment: bonds rallied on the jobs miss, stocks rallied too, but oil is rising again. The practical question for wealth advisors is asset allocation — specifically, whether the record-high equity allocation is justified when a single CPI print could reprice both legs of the portfolio. Know how to explain rate sensitivity of bond durations and equity multiples in plain language.

Equity Research

The Palantir story this week is a masterclass in growth-stock analysis: a company reporting Q2 revenue of $1.94 billion (up 93% YoY) and adjusted EPS of $0.41 versus a $0.35 consensus — yet still trading at extreme multiples that require sustained growth to justify. Analysts must distinguish between operational momentum and valuation sustainability. Interviewers will expect you to know both.

Investment Banking

SpaceX's first quarterly earnings report as a public company — $7.8 billion in revenue, up 92% YoY — is an M&A and capital markets primer. The company's lock-up expiration on August 6 (releasing 911.5 million insider shares worth roughly $100 billion) is a live case study in IPO mechanics, post-lock-up price dynamics, and how equity overhang affects deal pricing. Be ready to discuss this in any technology or aerospace banking interview.

07 Why This Matters for Interviews

The Question Behind the Question: Can You Think Like a Fed Watcher?

  • Likely question: "The July jobs report showed payrolls fell 23,000 but unemployment dropped to 4.1%. How should the Fed interpret that?"
  • Strong answer: The headline decline is partially distorted by seasonal adjustments and downward revisions. The Fed will focus on the unemployment rate decline as a signal that labor market slack hasn't widened dramatically — which means inflation could stay sticky even as hiring slows. Chair Warsh has signaled a data-dependent, hawkish-leaning posture; expect him to stay on hold in September but keep a hike on the table pending CPI.
  • Follow-up angle: "What's the bigger risk to markets this week — a hot CPI or a cold one?" A hot CPI is the sharper near-term risk: it directly reverses the jobs-miss trade that drove Friday's rally. A cold CPI is better for equities but could mask building energy-side inflation from oil near $80.
  • Don't say: "The Fed will cut rates because jobs were weak." The Fed under Warsh is not in a cutting cycle — it is in a hold-with-hike-bias cycle. Confusing the direction of policy is an instant credibility hit in any macro interview.
08 Term of the Day
Fed Reaction Function

The Fed reaction function describes how the Federal Reserve adjusts monetary policy in response to changes in economic variables — primarily inflation and employment. In practice, it means: if inflation rises above target, the Fed tightens (hikes rates); if the labor market weakens materially, it eases (cuts) or holds. Today's relevance: Friday's jobs miss shifted the inputs to the reaction function — weaker employment — but the inflation input (CPI, due Wednesday) hasn't updated yet. Until it does, markets are pricing a reaction function that may be based on incomplete data. That's exactly why Wednesday's print carries outsized weight.
