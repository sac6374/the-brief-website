Record Highs Meet a CapEx Reality Check: What Comes Next

Wall Street hit fresh records Tuesday — the S&P 500's first in two months — as oil prices collapsed on Strait of Hormuz deal hopes and Palantir's blowout quarter reignited AI enthusiasm. But the after-hours session delivered a cold shower: SpaceX and AMD both beat estimates and both fell hard, reminding investors that revenue growth alone no longer gets you a pass when capital expenditures are in the tens of billions. This morning, futures are steady, Asia is ripping, and the market is waiting on one thing: whether Washington and Tehran can actually close the Hormuz deal today.

00 Smart in 30 Seconds
The whole issue in a paragraph

Tuesday delivered a historic session: the S&P 500 closed at 7,737 (+1.79%), its first record in two months, while the Dow crossed 54,000 for the first time ever (+1.71%), powered by Palantir's 29% single-day surge after an AI blowout quarter, a 5%-plus collapse in oil on Strait of Hormuz deal optimism, and a 4%+ rally in the technology sector (XLK). After the bell, SpaceX posted Q2 revenue of $7.8B (vs. $6.93B expected, +92% YoY) and AMD hit record quarterly revenue of $11.5B — but both stocks fell roughly 8% in extended trading as investors fixated on SpaceX's $18.4B quarterly capex figure, more than double its Q1 level. This morning, S&P 500 and Dow futures are up ~0.2%, Asia is broadly higher (Nikkei +3.7%, Kospi +3.8%), oil is bouncing near $76 on a Houthi attack on a Saudi vessel in the Red Sea, and a 60-day Hormuz interim deal could be announced as early as today — making today's session a live geopolitical event.

01 What Moved Markets Today

Three Catalysts, One Direction: Records Across the Board

Geopolitics / Oil

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC on Tuesday morning that the U.S. and Iran could reach a deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz "today or tomorrow," citing freedom of movement for commercial ships. WTI crude settled down more than 5% to approximately $75.77/barrel — its lowest close since mid-July — as the market priced in the end of a 158-day blockade that had disrupted roughly 2.6 billion barrels of global crude supply. Lower oil directly compressed inflation expectations, and Treasury yields slipped further from their July 31 year-to-date high of 4.73%, easing the discount rate on growth stocks.

AI Earnings — Palantir (PLTR)

Palantir surged 29% on Tuesday, extending a 17% premarket move from Monday after the company delivered what management called "otherworldly" AI software growth and raised its full-year outlook. The move pulled the broader AI complex higher, with the tech sector (XLK) jumping more than 4% on the day and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index (SOX) rising more than 7% — its fourth straight session of gains, rallying over 1,800 points off its July 29 low of 10,445.

After-Hours — SpaceX (SPCX) & AMD

SpaceX reported its first-ever quarterly earnings as a public company: Q2 revenue of $7.81B beat the $6.93B consensus by 13%, and adjusted EBITDA of $3.5B nearly doubled analyst expectations. AMD posted record Q2 revenue of $11.5B (+50% YoY) with Data Center sales up 107% YoY to $6.7B. Despite both beating estimates, SPCX fell ~8.6% in extended trading to ~$114.60 and AMD fell ~8–9%, as investors focused on SpaceX's $18.4B quarterly capex — more than sixfold higher than a year earlier and above the $13.22B analyst estimate.

02 Why It Mattered

The Market Just Reset the Bar for What a "Beat" Actually Means

For most of earnings season, beating revenue and EPS estimates was enough to send stocks higher. SpaceX and AMD changed that calculus Tuesday night. SpaceX grew revenue 92% year-over-year — an extraordinary number for a company of its scale — and still got sold. The reason: $18.4B in a single quarter of capital expenditure, with $15.8B of that dedicated to AI infrastructure. That figure more than doubled Q1's $7.7B capex, and it arrived at a moment when the entire market is scrutinizing whether AI infrastructure spending will ever generate commensurate returns.

This isn't isolated to SpaceX. Over the last two weeks, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft, and Amazon all reported massive AI capex, and the pattern has been consistent: investors are increasingly willing to punish huge capex even when the underlying business is growing fast. AMD's situation is subtly different — its Data Center segment grew 107% YoY and the guidance was strong — but the stock's after-hours reaction suggests that any company touching AI hardware right now faces a high bar. The market wants margin expansion, not just revenue growth financed by ever-larger investment cycles.

The broader session, however, remained a clear win. The S&P 500's record close was its first since early June. The Dow crossed 54,000 for the first time in history. The PHLX Semiconductor Index is now up more than 1,800 points in five trading days — a remarkable recovery from late-July's tech selloff driven by fears that AI spending wasn't generating returns. Tuesday's session proved that a Hormuz deal, falling yields, and a handful of the right earnings results can override sector-level anxiety very quickly.

Note: AMD's adjusted EPS of $1.66 reflects 246% YoY growth — a massive beat. However, per editorial standards, any beat of this magnitude should be understood in context: AMD's EPS base was severely depressed a year ago due to restructuring costs and a weak data center cycle. The operational momentum is real, but the percentage comparison overstates the normalized improvement.

04 What It Means

A Pivotal 24 Hours: Hormuz Deal, Fed Odds, and the AI Capex Debate

For Rates

The 10-year yield has pulled back roughly 10 basis points from its July 31 YTD high of 4.73%, settling near 4.63%. The Fed held rates steady in July, but three policymakers dissented — and markets are pricing roughly a 65% probability of a 25bp hike in September. A sustained decline in oil prices, if a Hormuz deal holds, would directly reduce energy-driven inflation and could shift that odds calculation lower, giving the Fed political cover to stay on hold.

For Equities

Record closes mask a bifurcating market. Palantir and Caterpillar thrived; Nike lagged. The after-hours reaction to SpaceX and AMD — both genuine beats — warns that the AI capex tolerance trade is getting crowded. Stocks with massive infrastructure spend under scrutiny include Alphabet, Meta, Oracle, and Microsoft. A Hormuz deal would boost industrials, transports, and energy refiners, broadening the rally beyond tech.

For the Economy

The Strait of Hormuz has been blocked for 158 days, disrupting roughly 2.6 billion barrels of global crude supply. A reopening would gradually ease global energy prices, reduce shipping costs, and lower headline inflation — potentially improving real consumer purchasing power heading into Q3. For the U.S., lower energy costs are a direct input into ISM manufacturing data, transportation margins, and consumer sentiment indices.

What to Watch

Three things matter most today: (1) any formal announcement from Washington or Tehran on the Strait of Hormuz deal — watch crude futures in real time; (2) how SPCX and AMD trade at the open after their AH declines; and (3) the JOLTS job openings report, which gives the Fed another data point ahead of Friday's nonfarm payrolls. A weak JOLTS print could soften September rate-hike odds meaningfully.

Key Watch Item

The Hormuz interim deal. Per Axios and TradingEconomics, the U.S., Iran, and Oman are close to a 60-day agreement to reopen the strait without tolls — and Washington is aiming to announce it as early as Wednesday. If confirmed, this is the single largest potential catalyst remaining in the current macro environment: oil could fall another $5–10/barrel, yields could drop further, and equities would likely extend their record run.

05 Market Sector Lens

Tech and Industrials Lead; Defensives Lag as Risk-On Dominates

TECHNOLOGY (XLK) +4%+

The tech sector was the clear session leader, driven by Palantir's 29% single-day gain, a broad semiconductor rally (SOX +7%-plus), and continued momentum in AI platform names. The Nasdaq-100 gained 3.27% on the day. The tech recovery now extends four straight sessions and has erased the bulk of July's selloff.

ENERGY (XLE) Lagged

Energy stocks face a structural headwind as WTI fell more than 5% in a single session on Hormuz optimism. E&P names and integrated majors declined even as the broader market rallied. If a deal is formalized today, energy could see another leg lower — though refinery and chemical names may benefit from lower feedstock costs.

INDUSTRIALS (XLI) Watch

Industrials hit all-time highs Tuesday, led by Caterpillar's 5.97% surge after raising revenue guidance. Financials also advanced, with Goldman Sachs up more than 2%. A Hormuz reopening would be an additional positive for global trade and supply chain names in this sector — making it the one to watch if the deal is confirmed today.

06 Finance Career Lens

What Tuesday's Session Teaches You About Market Narratives — and How Fast They Shift

Across all tracks

Tuesday is a case study in how multiple catalysts can align simultaneously — and how quickly the market reweights them. A macro geopolitical development (Hormuz), a sector earnings catalyst (Palantir/AI), and a rates signal (falling yields) all pointed in the same direction at once. That convergence is rare. Knowing how to identify when catalysts are stacking — versus when they're offsetting — is a core analytical skill.

Wealth Management & Private Banking

Record equity closes alongside elevated VIX (16.50) create a natural client conversation point: the rally is real, but volatility hasn't gone away. Clients with overweight energy positions need reassessment given the Hormuz dynamic. This is also a moment to revisit duration risk in fixed income — the 10-year has moved 10+ basis points in two weeks on geopolitical news alone, not Fed action.

Equity Research

The SpaceX and AMD after-hours reactions are a masterclass in "priced for perfection" dynamics. Both companies delivered genuine beats on revenue and earnings — yet both sold off, because capex guidance overwhelmed the income statement. For analysts covering AI infrastructure names, the model question is no longer "will revenue grow?" but "when does the capex cycle peak, and what does free cash flow look like on the other side?" Build your FCF bridge carefully.

Investment Banking

SpaceX's debut quarterly report as a public company — following its record IPO in June — is a live case study in IPO aftermarket dynamics and public market expectation-setting. The stock is down 16% from its $150 IPO open price as of Tuesday's close, despite a 92% revenue beat. For IB candidates, this is a talking point on the tension between IPO valuation and ongoing earnings transparency: growth-stage companies face a structural re-rating process once public financials are disclosed quarterly.

07 Why This Matters for Interviews

The Question an Interviewer Will Ask You About This Week

  • The likely question: "SpaceX beat revenue estimates by 13% and EBITDA estimates by 75%, but the stock fell 8% after-hours. How do you explain that?"
  • What a strong candidate says: "The market is re-pricing on free cash flow expectations, not just revenue growth. With $18.4B in quarterly capex — more than double Q1 — investors are discounting the near-term FCF generation and asking whether the AI infrastructure spend will earn above the cost of capital. At SpaceX's current capex trajectory, you're looking at $70B+ in annualized capex, which is difficult to justify even with $100B in projected annualized revenue unless margins expand significantly."
  • A strong follow-up angle: Compare this to a prior capex-heavy cycle — Amazon in 2014–2016, or Intel's fab investment in 2021 — and discuss how markets eventually rewarded the spend once returns materialized. Frame it as a timing question, not a viability question.
  • What NOT to say: Don't call it a simple "sell the news" event without quantifying the capex concern. Interviewers will push back if you can't explain the specific number that spooked the market.
08 Term of the Day
Capital Expenditure (CapEx) Overhang

Capital expenditure overhang refers to a situation where a company's announced or reported capital spending is so large relative to its near-term earnings power that it suppresses the stock price, even in the presence of strong revenue growth. Investors price in diluted future free cash flow because heavy capex consumes cash before it generates returns. Tuesday's SpaceX reaction is a textbook example: Q2 capex of $18.4B compared to Q2 revenue of $7.8B means the company spent more than twice its quarterly revenue on infrastructure investment — a ratio that markets are currently unwilling to look through without clearer return visibility.

09 Say This Today

Sound Sharp in Any Finance Conversation This Morning

If someone asks about the SpaceX selloff despite a revenue beat:

"It's a capex credibility story at this point. SpaceX grew revenue 92% year-over-year and still fell 8% after-hours because quarterly capex hit $18.4 billion — more than twice what it spent in Q1. The market has effectively set a new rule for AI infrastructure plays: you need to show a credible path to free cash flow, not just revenue growth. The question now is whether that threshold is structural or whether it shifts once a Hormuz deal lowers energy costs and rates come off."
