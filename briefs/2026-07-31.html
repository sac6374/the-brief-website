00 Smart in 30 Seconds
The whole issue in a paragraph

Wall Street staged a powerful comeback Thursday — the S&P 500 surged 1.7% to 7,437.63, the Nasdaq jumped 2.8% to 25,122.18, and Microsoft posted the largest single-day market-cap gain in history after Azure-driven earnings crushed estimates. Then after the close, Amazon and Apple both reported record June quarters — but Wall Street split them immediately: Amazon's AWS grew 37% YoY to $42.2 billion, the fastest pace in 18 quarters, sending shares up 12%+ premarket; Apple beat on revenue and EPS but issued Q4 guidance below consensus, dragging shares down ~8%. All of this is playing out against a treacherous rate backdrop: the 10-year Treasury yield climbed to an 18-month high Friday morning, as the bond market continues to question the Fed's inflation resolve after Chair Kevin Warsh held rates steady Wednesday with three FOMC members dissenting in favor of a hike. Friday's session will try to hold Thursday's gains while absorbing the Amazon/Apple split and surging yields — an increasingly difficult balancing act.

01 What Moved Markets Today

Microsoft Led a Megacap Stampede; Amazon and Apple Set Up a Divided Friday Open

Thursday's Session — Tech Rebound

Thursday's rally was built on one number: Microsoft's 16% single-session surge, which added roughly $450 billion to its market value — the largest single-day market cap gain by any stock in history. Azure growth validated the AI infrastructure thesis after Wednesday's Fed-driven selloff had wiped out nearly $1.2 trillion in market value. The iShares Semiconductor ETF climbed over 8% on the day, and the Nasdaq 100 advanced 3.4%, ending a six-day losing streak. Notably, however, nearly two-thirds of S&P 500 components still traded lower — the rally was narrow, concentrated in the biggest names.

Thursday After-Hours — Amazon vs. Apple

Amazon reported Q2 revenue of $200.6 billion (+19.6% YoY) with AWS at $42.2 billion, its fastest expansion since 2021. EPS came in at $5.75 versus the $1.82 consensus estimate — a gap that large almost certainly includes one-time, non-operating items (investment gains and similar), so the operational beat is far smaller than the headline suggests. Amazon also raised its full-year 2026 capex plan to $220 billion. Apple posted fiscal Q3 revenue of $109.4 billion, beating the $108.65 billion estimate, with EPS of $2.02 vs. $1.89 expected — but its Q4 revenue guidance of $111.7–$113.7 billion fell short of the $114.84 billion consensus. Shares fell roughly 8% in after-hours; Amazon surged 12%.

Rates & Fed Backdrop

The 10-year Treasury yield advanced Friday morning to its highest level since January 2025. The 30-year bond yield hovered near 5.26%, its highest since 2007. These moves stem from Wednesday's FOMC meeting, where the Fed held rates at 3.5%–3.75% for the fifth consecutive meeting — but three members dissented in favor of a rate hike, the most dissents in the same direction since September 2016. University of Michigan consumer sentiment for July came in at 55.2, better than the 54.0 consensus estimate but still 10.5% below a year ago.

02 Why It Mattered

The AI Trade Is Alive — But It's Getting Highly Selective

Thursday's session was the market's answer to a question that had been building all week: does AI spending actually show up in revenue? Microsoft said yes — loudly. Azure's growth convinced dip buyers to pile back in after Wednesday's Fed-driven rout, and the semiconductor complex followed. But the after-hours prints from Amazon and Apple reveal something more nuanced: the AI theme is not rising tide that lifts all boats. It is creating a bifurcation between companies that monetize AI demand through cloud infrastructure and those constrained by the AI-driven chip shortage on the hardware side.

Amazon is the clearest AI monetization story in megacap tech right now. AWS growing at its fastest clip in four and a half years — with CEO Andy Jassy declaring AI and chips businesses each running at over $25 billion annualized run rates — makes the case that the hyperscaler capex supercycle is generating returns. Amazon also raised its full-year capex plan again — a signal that management sees the demand as durable. Analysts at Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, and Barclays responded with price target hikes, calling it one of the cleanest quarters in years.

Apple's story is more complicated. The headline numbers were fine — even strong — but the Q4 guidance shortfall is hard to dismiss. Apple is grappling with a chip shortage that is constraining production of its core hardware products, while the services segment, the growth engine investors prize most, showed some softness. Gross margin on devices fell 200 basis points quarter-over-quarter. Mac and iPad prices have already risen; iPhone price increases are expected later this year. The irony is that the same AI chip demand that is making Amazon rich is eating into Apple's ability to manufacture at scale.

Context: The Fed held its benchmark rate at 3.5%–3.75%, unchanged for the fifth straight meeting. Three FOMC members — Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari, and Dallas's Lorie Logan — voted to hike instead. Chair Kevin Warsh acknowledged inflation is still "elevated" but declined to provide clear forward guidance, saying only, "We've got no magic wand." The bond market interpreted the ambiguity as insufficient commitment to fighting inflation — hence the yield surge.

04 What It Means

A Divided Fed, Elevated Yields, and a Bifurcated Tech Rally: The Implications

For Rates

Yields at these levels represent a significant tightening of financial conditions even without a Fed hike. The three hawkish FOMC dissents signal that the next move from the Fed is more likely a hike than a cut — market pricing is rapidly adjusting to that reality. Warsh's refusal to provide forward guidance leaves the bond market in a vacuum, and it is filling that vacuum with yield premiums.

For Equities

Higher long-term yields compress equity multiples — especially for growth and tech stocks, which derive more of their value from future earnings. The narrow breadth of Thursday's rally (two-thirds of S&P 500 components still fell) is a warning sign. If Amazon can hold its premarket gains while Apple's selloff is contained, the index may finish higher. But the math gets harder with the 10-year at an 18-month high.

For the Economy

Consumer sentiment rose to 55.2 in July — better than expected and up 11.5% from June — suggesting households are not yet panicking about inflation. But sentiment is still 10.5% below year-ago levels. One-year inflation expectations dipped to 4.2%, the lowest since March, while five-year expectations held at 3.3%. These numbers are consistent with a slow grind, not a crisis — but they are also not consistent with a Fed that can cut rates soon.

What to Watch

ExxonMobil earnings before Friday's open (consensus: $3.67 EPS on $98.07B revenue) will test whether energy's strength is durable as WTI sits near $86. The Monolithic Power Systems beat (+11.4% premarket) adds to the semi-conductor sector's momentum. Watch Apple's opening price — if it holds above $310, the damage is manageable. A break below $300 would weigh on the Nasdaq.

Key Watch Item

The 10-year Treasury yield at 4.73% is the most important number heading into Friday's session. If yields continue to climb toward 4.80% or beyond, equities will struggle to hold Thursday's gains regardless of earnings results — and the Fed's next move will increasingly be priced as a hike, not a cut.

CONSUMER TECH (Hardware) AAPL −8%

Apple is the clear loser of this earnings cycle's final night. Despite beating on revenue and EPS, the Q4 guidance shortfall and underlying narrative — chip shortages constraining production, device gross margins falling 200 bps QoQ, and services growth showing cracks — sent shares down ~8% premarket. Roblox tumbled 22.5% and GoDaddy fell 16.4% on separate guidance concerns, adding to the negative consumer-facing tech tone.

ENERGY Watch

WTI crude climbed back above $85.92 (+2.8%) as tensions in the Strait of Hormuz flared again — Iran's Revolutionary Guard struck tankers attempting passage under U.S. escort. ExxonMobil reports Q2 earnings before Friday's open. The energy sector was one of the only gainers on Wednesday's down day, and continued crude strength keeps inflation expectations elevated — directly complicating the Fed's calculus.

06 Finance Career Lens

When Earnings Season Ends With a Bang: What This Week Means for Your Career Track

Across all tracks

This week's earnings season finale — Microsoft, Amazon, Apple, Alphabet, Meta all reporting within days — is the single most information-dense period of the year. Knowing the specific numbers, understanding why the market reacted the way it did (not just that it did), and being able to articulate the macro backdrop (Fed, yields, geopolitics) is what separates candidates who read the headlines from those who understand markets.

Wealth Management & Private Banking

High-net-worth clients with concentrated tech positions are calling this week. The Microsoft surge and Amazon beat require a clear conversation: these moves are not aberrations — they reflect real AI revenue inflection — but the yield environment means valuations are being squeezed from both ends. Know how to discuss equity duration and why rising long-term yields hurt growth stocks even when earnings are strong. Apple's disappointing guidance is also a reminder that even the world's largest company is not immune to supply chain risk.

Equity Research

If you are targeting tech or consumer research, this week is your case study. The Amazon vs. Apple split illustrates how two companies with similar market caps and overlapping chip dependencies can produce radically different outcomes based on their positioning in the AI value chain. Practice building a relative value argument: AWS's accelerating growth with expanding margins versus Apple's device segment with contracting gross margins and supply constraints. That framing — with specific numbers — will land well in any interview.

Investment Banking

Amazon's decision to raise 2026 capex to $220 billion — from an already-elevated $200 billion — keeps the AI infrastructure M&A and financing pipeline very much alive. Data center developers, power utilities, chip equipment makers, and fiber networks are all downstream beneficiaries of hyperscaler capex. If you are recruiting for tech or power/utilities coverage, understand this capital flow. Meanwhile, Apple's guidance miss raises questions about whether a strategic pivot — more aggressive services monetization, a hardware subscription model — could be on the horizon, which is exactly the kind of strategic analysis bankers get paid to do.

07 Why This Matters for Interviews

The Questions This Week Will Generate in Every Finance Interview Room

  • "Walk me through what Amazon reported and why the market reacted the way it did." — The answer requires more than "it beat estimates." You need to explain AWS's growth acceleration in the context of the AI spending cycle, note that EPS of $5.75 crushed the $1.82 consensus — while flagging that a 3x beat almost always contains one-time, non-operating items — and explain why the capex raise was received positively rather than as a red flag.
  • "Apple also beat — so why did the stock fall 8%?" — Strong answer: Markets trade on the forward guide, not the rearview mirror. Apple's Q4 revenue guidance of $111.7–$113.7 billion missed the $114.84 billion consensus. Combine that with device gross margins compressing 200 bps QoQ, chip supply constraints, and a services segment that underwhelmed, and you have a classic "good quarter, bad guide" reaction.
  • "The Fed held rates again. Does that mean rates are coming down soon?" — No. Three FOMC members dissented to hike. The 30-year yield is at its highest since 2007. The market is pricing the next Fed move as more likely a hike than a cut. Warsh has not provided clear forward guidance, and PCE inflation is still running at 4.1%.
  • Don't say: "The market is just overreacting" or "tech always bounces back." Interviewers want rigorous cause-and-effect reasoning, not reassurance.
08 Term of the Day
Bear Steepener

A bear steepener occurs when long-term Treasury yields rise faster than short-term yields, widening the spread between them — a pattern associated with inflation fears or a credibility gap at the central bank. This week is a textbook example: the 10-year yield climbed from roughly 4.59% Monday to above 4.73% by Friday, while the 30-year surged toward 5.26%. When the bond market doesn't trust that a central bank will contain inflation — as it appears not to trust Warsh after Wednesday's hold — it demands higher compensation for holding long-duration debt, steepening the curve from the long end. For equity investors, a bear steepener is particularly dangerous because it raises the discount rate for future earnings, hitting growth stocks hardest.
