01 What Moved Markets Today

Trump Cancels Iran Strike: Oil Down 6%, Futures Up, Yields Retreat

Geopolitics / Oil

Over the weekend, President Trump announced he was calling off a planned military strike on Iran and that fresh diplomatic talks would begin Monday afternoon. WTI crude fell more than 6% this morning to approximately $79.50/barrel — the contract's largest single-day drop in absolute terms in weeks — reversing part of a 20%-plus rally last month driven by Strait of Hormuz disruptions. Brent crude dropped roughly 5% to about $83.50/barrel. Iran's foreign ministry, however, said it is not directly negotiating with Washington and is instead pursuing a separate shipping corridor with Oman — a wrinkle markets are watching closely.

Equities Premarket

S&P 500 futures rose approximately 0.6% and Dow futures climbed about 1.1% before the open, as lower oil prices cut inflation expectations and lifted sentiment. The 10-year Treasury yield fell to 4.67%, down roughly 8 basis points from Friday, easing some of the rate-hike anxiety that had gripped markets since the Fed's divided July 29 meeting. The Nikkei 225 dropped 0.9% in Asia after the U.S. and Japan jointly intervened to support the yen, which had traded near 164 yen per dollar — a 40-year high — before pulling back to around 156 by late Monday Asia time.

Friday Recap: What You Missed

The last session ended Friday July 31, with all three major indexes in the green. Amazon was the star of the day, jumping 15.3% (YoY) after reporting strong cloud growth in its Q2 earnings. Alphabet gained 6.9% and Microsoft added 3% on AI infrastructure optimism. On the losing side, Apple fell 7.1% after chip shortages raised production costs and weighed on its June-quarter results. The S&P 500 rose 0.70%, the Nasdaq +1.00%, and the Dow +0.53%. For the full week, the S&P 500 gained approximately 1.0% (WoW) and the Nasdaq climbed roughly 1.6% (WoW). For July as a whole, however, the S&P 500 slipped 0.1% (MoM) and the Nasdaq fell 3.2% (MoM).

02 Why It Mattered

The Geopolitical Risk Premium Was Never Fully Priced In — Now It's Being Priced Out

Oil crossed $85/barrel last month on a single thesis: that the Strait of Hormuz — through which roughly 20% of the world's oil supply flows — was dangerously close to being shut. Tanker attacks, U.S. military positioning, and escalating rhetoric pushed energy markets to price in a scenario where supply disruptions were not just possible but imminent. That premium is now unraveling on a single Sunday statement from the president. That's the nature of geopolitical risk premia: they inflate fast on fear and deflate fast on hope, even before any deal is actually done.

And the hope here is thin. Iran's foreign ministry publicly denied it is engaged in direct U.S. negotiations. What Tehran is pursuing is a separate arrangement with Oman to establish a temporary shipping corridor — not a formal ceasefire or nuclear deal. Markets, however, are trading the headline, not the footnote. That gap between what was said and what is actually agreed creates meaningful downside risk to the oil rally unwinding — if talks collapse, the fear trade returns just as fast.

For equities, lower oil is unambiguously good in the short run: it reduces input costs across industrials and consumer goods, cools inflation expectations, and gives the Fed room to breathe. The 10-year yield dropping to 4.67% today reflects exactly that chain. Three Fed officials dissented at the July 29 meeting in favor of raising rates; a sustained pullback in energy prices could shift that calculus materially before the September FOMC meeting, where markets currently price a roughly 63% probability of a 25-basis-point hike.

The Strait of Hormuz context: Tanker traffic through the strait dropped to an average of 13 vessels per day in late July, compared to a normal average of roughly 33 per day. That alone explains the scale of last month's oil spike — and the scale of relief now being priced in.

04 What It Means

Lower Oil Changes the Calculus on Rates, Earnings, and the Fed's Next Move

For Rates

The 10-year yield is at 4.67% this morning, retreating from levels that were pushing toward 18-month highs last week. Lower energy prices directly reduce headline CPI expectations. If oil stays suppressed, the three hawkish Fed dissenters — who warned at the July 29 meeting that waiting risks more aggressive future tightening — may find it harder to build a coalition for a September hike. Markets currently price a ~63% probability of a 25-bps hike; that number is fluid.

For Equities

Lower oil broadly benefits S&P 500 earnings estimates by reducing input costs across consumer goods, industrials, and transportation. Energy sector stocks, however, will face direct earnings headwinds if WTI stays below $80. Airlines and logistics names are the clearest early beneficiaries. The market remains 1.6% below its June 2 all-time high — a sustained oil pullback removes one of the key arguments for remaining cautious.

For the Economy

Gasoline prices flowing from a sustained WTI pullback would effectively act as a tax cut for consumers — particularly meaningful at a moment when U.S. GDP growth printed at just 1.5% (annualized) last quarter and consumer savings have been depleted. The flip side: the GDP signal is already weak enough that even a 63% market-implied rate hike in September seems aggressive. A soft jobs report Friday would sharpen that tension considerably.

What to Watch

The actual content of U.S.-Iran talks — if they materialize — is the single most important variable. A deal covering Hormuz transit rights would accelerate the oil price decline and potentially remove 50+ basis points of risk-premium inflation from bond markets. A collapse in talks reverts every trade above in reverse. Monday afternoon is the first real test of whether Trump's weekend statement was a strategic opening or a tactical delay.

Key Watch Item

Iran's Foreign Ministry has not confirmed direct U.S. negotiations as of Monday morning — it is separately pursuing an Oman-mediated shipping corridor. Any official Iranian denial of talks would likely snap oil back higher and compress today's equity rally. Watch statements from Tehran closely through the afternoon session.

05 Market Sector Lens

Energy Gives, Consumer and Tech Take — A Classic Geopolitical Rotation

Consumer Discretionary & Industrials Tailwind

A 6%+ drop in WTI directly lowers input and transportation costs for consumer-facing companies. Airlines, logistics operators, and auto manufacturers see fuel-cost relief. On Friday, Amazon's 15.3% surge — driven by cloud growth rather than retail — already signaled that mega-cap discretionary is leading this market cycle. Lower energy is an additional tailwind for the sector's cost structure heading into Q3 earnings.

Energy (XLE) Headwind

Energy was the top-performing sector last month as WTI climbed more than 20% (MoM) on Hormuz fears. That premium is now reversing. ExxonMobil already fell 1% Friday as refinery capacity constraints limited its ability to benefit from the high-oil environment. With WTI sliding below $80, integrated oil majors and E&P companies face direct downward earnings revisions for Q3. Watch for pressure on XLE constituents at the open.

Technology Watch

Tech's picture is bifurcated. Cloud and AI infrastructure names — Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Nvidia — had a strong week as earnings validated AI capex narratives. But Apple's 7.1% Friday drop on chip-shortage-driven cost pressures is a reminder that hardware-dependent tech is vulnerable. SpaceX (SPCX) reporting Tuesday is the sector's next big event — and with a $100B+ lockup expiration Thursday, the outcome could move the broader Nasdaq meaningfully in either direction.

06 Finance Career Lens

How This Week's Confluence of Events Shows Up Across Every Finance Track

Across all tracks

This week is a textbook example of what finance professionals mean when they talk about "macro overlay" — a single geopolitical development (Iran diplomacy) simultaneously reprices oil, yields, equities, currencies, and corporate earnings expectations. Understanding how these chains connect is a foundational skill for any finance career, and being able to explain it clearly in an interview — or a client meeting — is what separates candidates who understand markets from those who just follow them.

Wealth Management & Private Banking

Clients holding energy-heavy portfolios after last month's oil rally need a conversation this week. The question is whether to take profits, rebalance, or hold through potential diplomatic volatility. More broadly, lower oil and a retreating 10-year yield (now 4.67%) shifts the calculus on fixed income duration — bonds are less punished at these levels, which reopens conversations about extending duration in client portfolios that may have been defensively short. The July jobs report Friday will be the key input for those duration calls.

Equity Research

Analysts covering energy names need to model at least two oil scenarios this week: one where diplomacy succeeds and WTI stabilizes in the $75–80 range, and one where talks collapse and the $85–90 risk-premium scenario reasserts. For tech analysts, Tuesday's SpaceX earnings are the most consequential single-stock event of the summer — the first public look at financials for a company trading at a $1.5 trillion market cap. Analysts need to stress-test consensus Q2 revenue estimates of $6.9B (YoY growth of ~68%) against reported actuals, and flag the lockup expiration as a structural supply-demand dynamic separate from fundamentals.

Investment Banking

Deal activity in the energy sector will be highly sensitive to where oil settles. M&A conversations that assumed sustained high oil prices may need to be revisited. Separately, SpaceX's IPO — the largest in history at $85.7B raised — is the benchmark deal of 2026, and its post-IPO performance (down 45%+ from peak) will shape how banks pitch and price the next generation of mega-tech listings. Understanding the lock-up mechanics, float dynamics, and how they interact with earnings catalysts is directly relevant for anyone recruiting into ECM (equity capital markets) or tech banking.

07 Why This Matters for Interviews

SpaceX, Lockups, and the Mechanics of Post-IPO Markets

  • Question you might get: "SpaceX is down 45% from its IPO peak. Is this a buying opportunity or a structural problem?" — This is a live version of a classic valuation vs. technicals interview trap.
  • Strong answer framework: Separate the fundamental story from the technical overhang. Fundamentals: Q2 consensus revenue of $6.9B implies 68% YoY growth; analyst expectations for ~100% growth in 2026 and 2027 are the core bull thesis, supported by Starlink subscriber growth and a nascent AI data center business. Technical overhang: 911.5 million shares (~12% of total, more than the current entire public float) unlock on August 6. That supply event can pressure price independent of fundamentals — and historically, IPO stocks underperform in the year following debut.
  • Follow-up angle: "How would you think about the fair value of a company reporting its first-ever public financials?" — The answer should address DCF limitations with early-stage cash flow-negative companies, and the role of comparable multiples (revenue or EV/EBITDA on forward estimates) as an alternative anchor. Also note SpaceX posted a net loss of $4.28B in Q1 2026 on $4.69B revenue — so any 'blowout' EPS beat relative to consensus requires scrutiny of whether it reflects operating improvement or one-time items.
  • What NOT to say: Don't conflate a stock declining 45% from its high with the company being "cheap." Price movement is not valuation. Always distinguish between the stock and the business.
08 Term of the Day
Geopolitical Risk Premium

A geopolitical risk premium is the extra price built into an asset — most commonly oil or safe-haven instruments like Treasuries or gold — that reflects the market's compensation for the probability of a disruptive geopolitical event. It is not based on supply-demand fundamentals alone; it is based on fear of what could happen. When that fear recedes — as it did this weekend when Trump announced a pause on Iran strikes — the premium deflates rapidly, which is exactly why WTI crude dropped more than 6% in a single session despite no actual change in physical supply. The premium had added an estimated 20%+ to oil prices over July; today, markets are partially giving that back on diplomatic hope alone.
