00 Smart in 30 Seconds
The whole issue in a paragraph

Tuesday's session was a textbook geopolitics-meets-macro trade: the S&P 500 fell 24.91 points (−0.32%) to 7,728.20, its second straight dip from last Friday's all-time high, as Iran's foreign minister declared there was "no possibility" of restarting Strait of Hormuz negotiations, keeping crude volatile near $82–$83/barrel and stoking inflation anxiety ahead of Wednesday's CPI. The 10-year Treasury yield eased to 4.68% from 4.71% as investors front-ran a benign inflation print — and they were right: July CPI came in this morning exactly at consensus, +0.1% MoM and 3.4% YoY, with core at +0.2% MoM and 2.5% YoY. After the bell, CoreWeave delivered a standout quarter — $2.58 billion in revenue, up 112% YoY, with a revenue backlog of $104.2 billion — sending shares up 14%+ in extended trading and injecting AI-infrastructure optimism into Wednesday's open. The Fed's September decision remains a live debate, and oil's next move on Hormuz diplomacy will likely determine whether CPI's tame reading holds.

01 What Moved Markets Today

Iran Impasse, Inflation Anxiety, and a Tech Selloff Pushed Stocks Lower for a Second Day

Geopolitics / Oil

The dominant driver on Tuesday was the breakdown in U.S.–Iran diplomacy over the Strait of Hormuz. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said there was "no possibility of restarting negotiations" under current conditions, while President Trump rebuked Tehran's demand for war reparations. Oil prices oscillated between $87 and $90 for Brent crude before settling around $88.91/barrel (up 1.4% on the day), sustaining investor anxiety about a renewed energy-driven inflation pulse. The Energy sector (XLE) outperformed sharply, rising approximately 4% on the session, the mirror image of everything else.

Equities / Big Tech

Alphabet (GOOG) sank 3.61%, the single largest drag on the Nasdaq and a key reason the composite underperformed the S&P 500. The Nasdaq fell 159.91 points (−0.60%) to 26,445.45, while the S&P 500 dropped 24.91 points (−0.32%) to 7,728.20 and the Dow shed 184.13 points (−0.34%) to 53,791.85. Rate-sensitive sectors led the retreat: Real Estate (XLRE) fell 1.2%, Utilities (XLU) dropped 1.1%, and Technology (XLK) slid 1.1%. Small-caps bucked the trend — the Russell 2000 rose 0.3% to 3,027, suggesting some rotation into domestically-focused names ahead of the CPI print.

Rates / Treasuries

Bond markets offered a small counterpoint to equity weakness. The 10-year Treasury yield pulled back to 4.68% after briefly rising above 4.71% on Monday, as investors positioned for a softer-than-feared CPI reading. Treasury prices edged up — a modest flight-to-quality bid — ahead of Wednesday's data. The move was measured, not dramatic, reflecting genuine uncertainty about whether the energy shock embedded in the prior months' prints had fully faded.

02 Why It Mattered

This Wasn't Just an Oil Story — It Was a Test of the Inflation Narrative

The Strait of Hormuz has been the single biggest macro variable in U.S. markets since it was effectively closed in early March following U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran. The waterway carries roughly a quarter of global oil flows, and its continued disruption sent crude oil surging to $120+ earlier this year — directly driving CPI to 4.2% in May. The slow retreat since then has been the market's lifeline. Tuesday's session was a reminder that this lifeline is fragile: one hardline statement from Tehran, and energy markets swing 5–7% in a session.

That fragility matters doubly because the Federal Reserve has explicitly tied its September rate decision to incoming inflation data. Markets entered Tuesday pricing roughly 50/50 odds of a Fed hike at the September 16 FOMC meeting. A CPI miss to the upside would have shifted that decisively toward a hike. A clean in-line print — which is exactly what arrived Wednesday morning — keeps the "no need to hike" camp alive, but it doesn't kill the hawk argument. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack wrote Tuesday that "now is the time to act," citing the risk that delayed tightening makes inflation harder and more expensive to defeat. The divide inside the Fed is real.

The after-bell news from CoreWeave added a different flavor. The AI cloud provider reported $2.58 billion in Q2 revenue, up 112% YoY, and guided 2026 revenue to $12.4–$13.2 billion — above prior consensus. Shares jumped 14%+ in extended trading. This matters beyond one stock: CoreWeave's backlog of $104 billion signals that hyperscaler and enterprise AI spending remains ferocious, which has read-through implications for Nvidia, data center REITs, and the broader infrastructure thesis heading into fall earnings season.

Note: CoreWeave's adjusted EPS loss of $1.03 beat expectations of a $1.20 loss. The company remains deeply unprofitable on a net income basis (Q2 net loss of $626 million), and the beat is on a loss-reduction basis — not an operating profit. Analysts should distinguish narrowing losses from operational breakeven before calling this a "profitability" story.

04 What It Means

In-Line CPI Buys Time, But Doesn't Buy Certainty

For Rates

The 10-year yield fell modestly to 4.68% on Tuesday, anticipating the benign print. With CPI landing exactly at consensus (3.4% YoY headline, 2.5% core), there is no fresh upward pressure on yields from today's report. But markets are pricing roughly 50/50 on a September hike — that won't resolve until August CPI and the jobs report land in September. Yields may stay rangebound between 4.5% and 4.75% unless Hormuz deal news dramatically shifts the energy-inflation trajectory.

For Equities

The in-line CPI removes a near-term downside risk (a hot print would have hammered multiples). That's constructive at the margin for growth and tech stocks, which are most sensitive to rate expectations. CoreWeave's 14%+ after-hours gain adds an AI-specific tailwind heading into Wednesday's open. However, the broader market has now posted two consecutive declines from its all-time high — momentum is slightly negative, and any Hormuz escalation can override CPI's relief.

For the Economy

Inflation at 3.4% YoY is still 140 basis points above the Fed's 2% target, and real wages remain negative — wage growth at 3.2% YoY is running below inflation. Consumers are still losing purchasing power. Shelter costs, though rising only 0.1% MoM in July, still accounted for roughly two-thirds of the headline CPI increase. The energy-driven price shock from the Iran war has moderated from its 4.2% peak in May, but the Hormuz situation means that moderation is reversible.

What to Watch

Thursday's PPI report is the next inflation read. Equally important: Hormuz diplomatic signals. Qatar's foreign ministry indicated negotiations are "at an advanced stage" — if that advances to a credible deal, oil could fall sharply, dragging energy CPI components with it and making a September hike significantly less likely. Watch crude oil futures as the real-time Fed policy proxy this week.

Key Watch Item

Cisco (CSCO) reports earnings Wednesday after the close — the first major legacy tech name this cycle. Its results will test whether AI-driven infrastructure spending is lifting enterprise networking, or whether IT budget cuts are offsetting gains. A beat could reinforce the CoreWeave-driven AI tailwind; a miss would introduce doubt.

05 Market Sector Lens

Energy Exploded Higher While Tech and Rate-Sensitives Took the Hit

ENERGY (XLE) +~4.0%

The clear winner on Tuesday. Continued Hormuz uncertainty and Iran's hardline stance kept crude near $83/barrel WTI, driving integrated oil companies and upstream E&P names sharply higher. National Energy Services (NESR) surged 23.3% after a strong earnings report — though that is a single-stock move, not an XLE figure. XLE's session gain reflects broad energy sector strength tied directly to supply disruption risk.

TECHNOLOGY (XLK) −1.1%

Rate sensitivity and Alphabet's 3.61% decline were the proximate causes. Higher-for-longer rate anxiety depresses the present value of future cash flows, which is the mechanism that hits growth and mega-cap tech hardest. Alphabet's specific move appeared tied to competitive pressures in AI and cloud, amplified by a risk-off mood. The after-hours CoreWeave surge could partially reverse XLK weakness at Wednesday's open.

REAL ESTATE (XLRE) Watch

XLRE fell 1.2% on Tuesday — the worst-performing sector — as yield anxiety weighed on rate-sensitive REITs. However, with CPI landing in-line and the 10-year yield easing, the near-term rate pressure may have peaked. Data center REITs in particular could catch a bid from CoreWeave's blowout backlog numbers, given that AI infrastructure buildout directly drives demand for colocation and power capacity.

06 Finance Career Lens

When Macro, Geopolitics, and Earnings All Hit at Once — Know Which Thread Your Desk Pulls

Across all tracks

Today is a recruiting interviewer's dream question setup: three major concurrent catalysts (CPI, Hormuz, CoreWeave earnings) pointing in different directions. The candidate who can separate the signal from the noise — and articulate which matters most for a specific portfolio mandate or deal thesis — immediately stands out.

Wealth Management & Private Banking

Client conversations today will center on two fears: "Is inflation coming back?" and "Should I be worried about energy stocks?" The in-line CPI print is the answer to question one — for now. For question two, help clients understand that XLE's 4% gain on Tuesday reflects a geopolitical risk premium, not fundamental demand growth, and that a Hormuz resolution could reverse those gains rapidly. Portfolios with energy overweights built on geopolitical bets need rebalancing frameworks, not momentum chasing.

Equity Research

CoreWeave's quarter is a comps goldmine for AI infrastructure coverage. Revenue up 112% YoY, backlog at $104.2 billion, and a raised full-year guide to $12.4–$13.2 billion create direct read-throughs for Nvidia (GPU demand), Equinix and Digital Realty (data center capacity), and even utilities (power demand). If you're building models on any of these names, today's CoreWeave print is a data point worth revisiting in your assumptions — particularly on revenue backlog conversion timelines.

Investment Banking

Two deal themes emerge from today's market. First, the Hormuz situation is accelerating energy infrastructure M&A conversations — alternative routing through Saudi pipelines (Yanbu), LNG terminal buildouts, and energy transition assets are all getting fresh sponsor attention. Second, the AI infrastructure capital markets window is wide open: CoreWeave's quarter demonstrates that institutional investors will absorb high-growth, loss-making AI infrastructure issuers. Expect follow-on equity raises and private credit deals in this space through year-end.

07 Why This Matters for Interviews

The CPI-Fed-Hormuz Triangle Is This Season's Most-Asked Setup

  • Q: "Walk me through how the Strait of Hormuz closure affects U.S. monetary policy." This is a chain-of-reasoning question. The answer runs: Hormuz closure → crude supply shock → energy prices spike → headline CPI rises → Fed faces inflation above target with a supply-side cause → traditional rate hikes can't fix supply problems, but the Fed may hike anyway if inflation expectations become unanchored.
  • Strong answer includes: the distinction between demand-pull and cost-push inflation, the Fed's dual mandate tension (inflation vs. employment), and the fact that September's decision is genuinely data-dependent with three more inflation prints before the meeting.
  • Follow-up angle: "If oil falls 10% tomorrow on a Hormuz deal, does that change your Fed call?" A strong candidate says yes — and explains that energy disinflation would push CPI toward 3.0% YoY quickly, shifting September pricing dramatically toward a hold.
  • Don't say: "The Fed will definitely hike" or "The Fed will definitely hold" — either definitive statement signals overconfidence. The honest, sophisticated answer is that the data remains genuinely uncertain, and the Fed has signaled it will respond accordingly.
08 Term of the Day
Cost-Push Inflation

Inflation caused by a rise in production costs — such as energy, raw materials, or wages — rather than by excess consumer demand. The key distinction from demand-pull inflation: monetary policy (raising interest rates) is a blunt tool against cost-push inflation because rate hikes reduce demand but cannot increase oil supply or reopen a blocked shipping lane. Today's CPI context is a direct illustration: headline inflation at 3.4% YoY is being shaped heavily by energy costs tied to the Strait of Hormuz closure — a supply disruption that the Federal Reserve's interest rate lever cannot directly fix, which is precisely why the September hike debate is so contested inside the FOMC.
